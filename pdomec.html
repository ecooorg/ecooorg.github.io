Path-Diverse Optical Mesh with Erasure Coding (PDOMEC): A Non-Line-of-Sight Tolerant Architecture for Free-Space Optical Inter-Satellite Links

1. Abstract

Free-space optical links in satellite constellations deliver high bandwidth but are vulnerable to short-duration blockages caused by orbital debris, atmospheric turbulence, cloud cells and pointing jitter. Conventional link-level retransmission (ARQ) fails on long inter-satellite baselines because the propagation delay exhausts buffers and collapses throughput.

This paper describes a framing-layer architecture that tolerates such blockages without retransmissions or mechanical re-pointing. Incoming data are collected into fixed-length frames, expanded by erasure coding (Reed-Solomon or rateless fountain codes) with a configurable over-provisioning ratio, and the resulting shards are injected simultaneously across multiple spatially separated optical terminals on the same node. The receiving node reconstructs the original frame at line rate as soon as any K out of N shards arrive, discarding the rest. Transient losses on individual paths are absorbed by the coding redundancy and by the geometric diversity of the optical axes.

The method is intended for high-capacity synchronous streams in LEO, MEO, GEO and deep-space optical mesh networks.

2. Background

Inter-satellite laser links routinely operate at multi-gigabit to terabit rates over distances of hundreds to thousands of kilometres. An optical beam is narrow and requires microradian pointing accuracy; even a few milliseconds of obstruction (debris cross-section, upper-atmosphere dip, or PAT transient) can erase millions of bits.

Retransmission-based recovery is impractical: the round-trip time on long baselines exceeds the buffer capacity of flight hardware, and the mechanical settling time of a laser terminal is orders of magnitude slower than the data rate. Pure path switching after a drop is detected therefore produces long outages.

What is needed is a proactive mechanism that assumes individual optical paths will fail on microsecond-to-millisecond timescales and that recovers the data without feedback or hardware re-alignment.

3. Related Work

Existing techniques address parts of the problem separately:

Erasure coding applied above the physical layer has been demonstrated for optical ground-to-satellite links to provide time diversity against scintillation fades.

Rateless (fountain) codes combined with parallel relays improve resource utilisation in terrestrial and satellite-to-ground FSO scenarios.

Reed-Solomon and LDPC forward-error correction are routinely used on single inter-satellite optical links; in-orbit experiments have shown multi-gigabit error-free performance.

Spatial diversity and multi-terminal mesh topologies increase connectivity, but traffic is normally routed on a single path at a time or simply duplicated.

Delay-tolerant networking handles long disruptions by store-and-forward, at the cost of latency and on-board storage.

The architecture described here differs in three respects: erasure coding is applied at the framing layer with an expansion ratio chosen for complete path outages rather than bit errors; distinct shards are injected simultaneously across multiple independent optical terminals of the same satellite; and reconstruction occurs the moment the mathematical threshold is reached, without acknowledgements or retransmission. This combination does not appear in the literature examined by the author.

4. Architecture

PDOMEC operates at the framing and physical-routing layers of each satellite node.

Frame accumulation

Incoming packets are buffered in high-speed SRAM for a deterministic interval Δt_frame (typically 50 µs to 2 ms). The resulting block is treated as a single payload of size K data units.

Erasure-coding expansion

A hardware encoder (FPGA or ASIC performing Galois-field arithmetic) produces N = K + M shards. Reed-Solomon codes give a fixed expansion; rateless fountain or random-linear network codes allow on-the-fly adjustment. The ratio N/K is set between 1.2 and 2.0 according to current debris-density models, atmospheric forecasts and measured link quality.

Path-diverse injection

Each node carries two or more independent optical terminals whose boresights are angularly separated in three-dimensional space. The N shards are distributed uniformly (or weighted by instantaneous link metrics) across the active terminals so that no single path carries a reconstructible subset. Distribution occurs at the framing layer and is independent of the higher-level routing tables; the physical axes therefore remain geometrically diverse even when the logical next hops change.

Threshold reconstruction

At the receiver the shards arrive out of order because of differential path lengths. They are collected in a reordering buffer. As soon as any K distinct shards have been received, the decoder executes the inverse matrix operation, reconstructs the original payload and forwards it at full line rate. Remaining shards are discarded. No acknowledgement is sent.

A practical validity test is:

Payload_Valid = (number of unique received shards ≥ K)

Out-of-order arrival is absorbed by the reordering buffer whose depth is sized to the maximum expected differential delay within the constellation.

5. Scope and Limitations

PDOMEC targets continuous high-rate streams:

inter-satellite backbone links in LEO, MEO and GEO constellations,

optical feeder links through turbulent atmosphere,

deep-space optical relay meshes.

It assumes the node has sufficient processing bandwidth and at least two optical terminals. It is not intended for single-terminal, power-constrained sensors. The scheme works on both single-hop and multi-hop paths; on multi-hop routes each intermediate node may optionally re-encode or simply forward the shards.

6. Implementation Notes

Δt_frame must be short enough to keep latency acceptable yet long enough for efficient coding blocks.

Encoding and decoding run on dedicated FPGA/ASIC fabric clocked directly from the transceiver data path to avoid CPU bottlenecks.

Angular separation between simultaneous optical paths should be maximised (contact-plan and geometry-aware selection) so that a single debris object or localised cloud cannot occult more than one terminal.

Expansion ratio and path set can be updated every few seconds from on-board debris catalogues or ground-uploaded forecasts.
