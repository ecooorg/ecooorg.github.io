BEFORE YOU CHOOSE: Find the Fact That Can Change the Decision

How to use AI for complex decisions without handing it the right to decide for you

UNDERSTAND → EXPAND → ATTACK → VERIFY → LEARN

Instead of asking “What should I choose?”, ask a different question: “What do I need to learn so that my choice could change?”

ZenodoTDCommonsFigshare

Abstract

Thesis. For complex personal and professional decisions, AI is most useful not as a source of answers but as a tool for finding what you do not know: it helps separate facts from assumptions, expand the set of options, find weak points in a plan, and design cheap tests before a mistake becomes costly or irreversible.

What is proposed. An operational framework of five loops (Bifurcation Engine), in which each loop provides material for the next, while the right to decide and the responsibility remain with the human. The framework builds on known techniques: pre-mortem, red team, value of information theory, the distinction between reversible and irreversible decisions, and a prediction journal.

What is not claimed. It is not claimed that the method has been shown to improve decision outcomes. Its components have their own evidence base, but their composition has not been tested experimentally. Section 13 describes a plan for such a test; Appendix D collects the method’s boundaries in one place.

What is new in this edition. Fully international wording (English, USD); no regional localization of contacts, currency, or legal references. “Quick start”: applicability triage and a minimal first pass in a few minutes. Three levels of description of the method (loops, Master Prompt stages, Decision Brief fields) are unified in one table at the end of Section 4. A single rule on the number of alternatives: three to five additional ones. Causes of mismatch between forecast and fact are described by a unified classification of five types, including “chance” (Section 10.2); the forecast in the journal is recorded by the human, not the model. The case includes a journal entry (Section 5.4); the Decision Brief has a “Decision taken” field.

Stages 3, 5, and 8 of the Master Prompt and the order of steps in Section 9 are clarified (the human fixes forecast, confidence, and thresholds before the test starts). The wording on source verification is aligned with what was actually checked.

Quick start: first pass

If you prefer not to read everything first, follow four steps. They take about ten minutes and give one minimal pass through the method. The rest of the text explains why the steps are structured this way and what to do next.

Step 1. Triage (about two minutes).

First, one check. If you are currently in acute crisis or severe distress, do not start with the protocol: you need a real person or a specialist (Section 12.1). If not, answer “yes” or “no” to four questions:

  • Will the error be costly: money, time, relationships, health?
  • Is the step hard or expensive to undo?
  • Are there substantial unknowns that can be resolved?
  • Will the consequences stretch for a long time — years, not weeks?

Then check against the table.

ResultWhat to do
You are in acute crisis or severe distressStop. You need a real person or a specialist, not a protocol. Postpone the decision if possible (Section 12.1).
The choice is driven only by values; nothing to discoverThe method in pure form does not apply. Work with values is needed: conversation, reflection.
Two or more “yes”The method is warranted. Proceed to step 2.

Step 2. Six fields (about five minutes).

Write a minimal version of the Decision Brief (Section 6): decision and deadline; cost of error and reversibility; critical hypotheses; experiment; kill criteria; review date. Decision, deadline, cost of error, and reversibility can be filled immediately. Hypotheses, experiment, and kill criteria will appear as the cycle proceeds. An empty field is normal: it shows what you do not yet know.

Step 3. First request (about two minutes).

Describe the situation in three to five sentences: what you are deciding, by which date, what constraints, what you do not know. Send the AI a message in the spirit of the case in Section 5:

Help me work through a decision, but do not choose for me. [Your situation in 3–5 sentences.] What do I need to learn before deciding? Questions only for now.

Do not write in it “I have decided…”, “I know for sure…”, or “confirm that I am right”: why that matters is explained in Section 3.2. Facts, including inconvenient ones, should be given fully.

Step 4. Answers and stop.

Answer the questions honestly: “I don’t know” is a legitimate and very useful answer. Then stop and look at the result: you have a labeled context and a list of unknowns (Loop 1). If among them there is a critical unknown that can be checked cheaply, the first pass has done its job. Next come expanding options, attack, and experiments: by the protocol in Section 9 with the Master Prompt (Section 8.2) or, if the decision is small, with one short request from Section 8.1.

1. The problem: why the question “What should I choose?” works poorly

The most common mistake when using AI for decisions is to use it as an oracle: state options A and B and ask what to choose. The model starts building coherent text inside the given frame. If critical facts are missing from the frame, it cannot add them from nothing.

Take the request: “I want to quit and open a coffee shop. Should I?” The model does not know the size of your savings and debts, lease terms, your food-service experience, family obligations, real demand in the area, or your readiness for operational load. The answer will be a generic essay on the risks of small business.

People increasingly turn to AI precisely for personal advice, so the quality of how the question is posed becomes a practical problem. Worse than a weak answer is something else: persuasive language creates the feeling that analysis was performed, even though analysis of your specific situation may not have happened. A framing error becomes invisible.

Therefore the goal of the method is not to make the model “decide better than a human”. The goal is to build a process in which the model’s strengths (structuring, enumerating options, critique, generating tests) are used where they are useful, and weaknesses are closed by external checks and human responsibility.

1.1. The core question and the name of the method

Core question of the Bifurcation Engine: “Which next fact or experiment would most change the decision — and how can that information be obtained cheaply, quickly, and safely?”

Why “Bifurcation”. Bifurcation is a fork, a point where the path diverges. The method works precisely with forks in life and work and does two things: it finds out which information will determine which branch to take, and it expands the fork from two branches to several (pilot, hybrid, conditional delay, reversible step).

Why “Engine”. This is not a one-off piece of advice but a repeatable cycle. It does not end with a decision: the last loop returns the result to the beginning so that the next decision is made better.

Two names. BEFORE YOU CHOOSE is the name of the publication; Bifurcation Engine is the name of the framework described in it.

The more costly the error and the harder it is to correct, the more independent verification and human control there should be. This dependence is formalized in Section 11.

2. What AI can do in the context of decisions — and what does not follow from that

A modern language model is not a “small person in a computer” and not mere autocomplete. It structures information, generalizes, finds patterns, writes code, compares options, and performs multi-step tasks. From that it does not follow that it has your life situation, personal responsibility, or a guarantee of truth.

For personal decisions it is useful to distinguish three things: what the model can analyze (structure, options, critique), what you are obliged to define yourself (values, acceptable risk, goal), and what the external world must confirm (facts, prices, rules, laws).

2.1. The model and the system around it

In practice it is important to distinguish the model from the system in which it is embedded. The model may receive documents, search, a calculator, code execution, and external services; an agent loop adds a cycle of “planning → action → observation → next step”. This expands capabilities and at the same time increases the surface of error.

RAG (retrieval-augmented generation) is an approach in which the model receives found fragments of external documents before answering. It helps with current and specialized information, but the found text does not become true by itself: source quality, search completeness, and correctness of interpretation matter. Therefore external sources are part of verification, not a substitute for it.

Most modern LLMs are built on the Transformer architecture with an attention mechanism and fine-tuned on human evaluations. For the method, the details of the architecture matter less than the consequence: the model is optimized to produce answers that are evaluated as good, and the evaluation of “good” does not always coincide with “true”.

3.1. Hallucinations: why “I don’t know” is rarely heard

Work by Kalai and co-authors in Nature explains why hallucinations are persistent. By their argument, this is not a mysterious failure but a predictable consequence of how models are trained and evaluated: standard tests score the share of correct answers and do not award points for abstaining, so guessing is more profitable than acknowledging uncertainty. The authors propose changing evaluation rules so that abstention and a penalty for confident error are explicit.

For the user the practical conclusion applied in the method by analogy follows: explicitly allow the model to answer “insufficient data” and explicitly state that a confident guess is worse than an acknowledged gap (see the prompt in Section 8). Caveat: the article itself proposes this for model evaluation; whether such wording in a user prompt reliably reduces hallucinations is not shown by it directly. This is a reasonable hypothesis, not an established fact.

3.2. Sycophancy: what the data show

Sycophancy is the tendency of a model to adjust answers to the user’s explicit or presumed position. It is documented in several assistants on different tasks and, according to the authors’ analysis, is partly linked to the fact that human evaluators more often prefer agreement. Conclusions should be held in three dimensions.

First: the wording of the request affects sycophancy. In controlled experiments, sycophancy was noticeably higher in response to assertions than to questions; it grew with the confidence demonstrated by the user and was amplified by first-person formulations (“I believe…”). Asking the model in advance to rephrase the user’s assertion as a question reduced sycophancy more than a simple instruction “don’t be a sycophant”. The authors note as an open question how far these effects transfer to multi-turn dialogues and deployed systems.

Second: sycophancy is not the same as worsening the decision. In one experiment the model was measurably sycophantic: it disproportionately often brought arguments in favor of the participant’s initial leaning and used flattering language. Nevertheless, on average the AI advice depolarized the choice, that is, moved participants away from their initial leanings and brought their decisions closer together. Strengthening sycophancy weakened this effect, so it is behaviorally significant but on average outweighed by the informativeness of the advice.

Third: limits of transfer. The tasks in that research are economic and social decisions in laboratory conditions, not your biography. It does not follow that in a personal decision with strong emotional involvement the effect will be the same. For the method the conclusion is: sycophancy is a reason to strengthen the verification process, not proof that any model answer is harmful.

4. Bifurcation Engine: five loops

Bifurcation Engine is not a separate model, not an autonomous agent, and not a magic prompt. It is an operational framework for human interaction with AI. Its originality is claimed not at the level of individual techniques: pre-mortem, red team, value of information, a decision journal, and the distinction between reversible and irreversible decisions exist independently. What is proposed as new is their combination into one repeatable cycle, where each loop creates material for the next, and the right to decide remains with the human. This is an authorial methodological synthesis, not an independently confirmed scientific methodology.

The loops describe the logic of the method. For work with AI they are broken down into nine stages of the Master Prompt (Section 8.2), and data for them accumulate in the Decision Brief (Section 6). The correspondence among the three levels is shown in the table at the end of this section.

Loop 1. UNDERSTAND

The task is to determine what exactly you are deciding and to build a map of knowledge. For this, the epistemic radar is used: labeling your context into six categories — confirmed facts; assumptions; interpretations; values and preferences; unknowns; claims requiring external verification.

Also determine the deadline, acceptable outcome, constraints, cost of error, and reversibility. If the model starts advising before receiving critical context, stop it and ask it first to ask questions: a recommendation built on incomplete data fixes the frame instead of checking it.

Loop 2. EXPAND

The task is to break the trap of binary choice. Look for a hybrid, a pilot, a temporary option, a reversible step, and an option “first obtain the critical fact”. A useful frame here is the distinction between decisions as a “two-way door” (you can go back) and a “one-way door” (return is costly or impossible); the term was popularized by Amazon and is used in management practice.

For each option ask two questions: which assumption must turn out to be true for it to work, and how much the exit will cost if it turns out to be false.

Volume rule. To the original A/B add from three to five options, among which a reversible step and the option “first get the fact” are mandatory. If you add fewer than three, the frame remains almost binary; if more than five, attention scatters and comparison becomes more expensive. This is a practical rule, not a research result; the same number is used in the loop table, in the short request (Section 8.1), and at stage 3 of the Master Prompt.

Loop 3. ATTACK

Ask the red team (the opposing team, in this case the role of the model) to attack first the preferred option, then the opposite one. Symmetry matters: without it, critique turns into justification of an already made choice. Then conduct a pre-mortem — Gary Klein’s method: imagine that in 12–24 months the plan has failed, and name the most plausible causes. Working “from the future backward” helps name risks that are awkward to voice in working mode.

The result of the loop is a list of vulnerable assumptions, from which you choose one to three critical hypotheses. Distinguish the testable from speculation: “rent will eat the margin” is testable; “someone won’t like it” is not.

Loop 4. VERIFY

For each critical hypothesis find a cheap test with high value of information (VoI). Principle: a test makes sense if the expected value of information is comparable to the cost of the test or higher, and if the result is capable of changing your decision. The theory goes back to the work of Howard and of Raiffa with Schlaifer.

HypothesisTestMetricWhat changes

Numeric example: how much is it worth to learn

Consider the hypothesis “there is sufficient demand in the area”. Suppose you estimate the probability that it is true at 40%. Gain for the first year if you open: +100 units if demand exists, and −150 if it does not. Refusing to open gives 0. (Units here are illustrative; use the same currency throughout — e.g. USD.)

  • Without additional information. Expected value of opening: 0.4 × 100 + 0.6 × (−150) = −50. Best move — do not open, value 0.
  • With perfect information. Open only if demand exists: 0.4 × 100 + 0.6 × 0 = 40.
  • Expected value of perfect information (EVPI) = 40 − 0 = 40. This is an upper bound on how much it makes sense to spend on finding out.

Implications. A test costing 10 is almost certainly justified, even if imperfect; a test costing 60 is not justified, even if perfect. Real tests give incomplete information, so their value is below EVPI. Limitations of the approach: probabilities and payoffs in personal decisions are estimated roughly; calculation in money assumes risk neutrality. If losing savings means more to you than an equivalent gain, you need to count in utility, not in money: the ranking of information sources depends on the utility function. For loss-averse decision-makers the ranking of tests can therefore change significantly. The numbers above are illustrative.

Loop 5. LEARN

Record the forecast before the result: what you expect, with what confidence, and on what grounds. You yourself record the forecast: AI can help formulate it in a testable way, but it does not substitute its confidence for yours, otherwise it is not your thinking that is being calibrated. After 30, 90, and 180 days compare the forecast with the fact. This counters hindsight bias: when the outcome is already known, it retrospectively seems more predictable than it was. Then determine the type of discrepancy: data, assumption, reasoning, execution, or chance — that determines what to change in the next cycle. Details are in Sections 10.1 and 10.2.

5. End-to-end case: coffee shop — from a bad question to an experiment

Status of the case. Below is a shortened dialogue composed by the author to illustrate the method. This is not a log of a real experiment, not proof of effectiveness, and not a forecast of coffee-shop success. AI replies are shortened; ellipses in square brackets mark omissions. Currency and amounts in the method are treated as generic units / USD-equivalent; no regional currency is assumed.

5.1. Initial framing

“I want to quit and open a coffee shop. Should I?” — a question to which a correct answer is impossible without a dozen unknowns. Let us see what happens with a different framing.

5.2. Dialogue

Loop 1: radar and questions

You: Help me work through a decision, but do not choose for me. I work as an analyst and am thinking of leaving to open a coffee shop in my neighborhood. Savings will cover roughly 5 months of living without income; I have a mortgage. What do I need to learn before deciding? Questions only for now.

AI: I will separate what was said. Facts: 5 months of savings, mortgage. Assumptions: there is demand in the neighborhood; operational work will suit you. Unknown: rent, your food-service experience, cost of opening, your monthly expenses. Questions whose answers could change the decision: (1) How much does opening cost and from which money — from the same five months? (2) What is the rent rate in suitable premises and what share of revenue will it take at realistic traffic? (3) Have you worked in food service for at least a few weeks? (4) What matters more to you — autonomy or stability, and what will “it worked” mean in a year? (5) What happens to the mortgage if income is interrupted for 6–8 months?

You: (1) Opening is roughly three of those five months, so about two remain for living. (2) I don’t know, I haven’t looked. (3) No. (4) Autonomy, but I cannot lose the mortgage. (5) The bank will most likely not grant forbearance, but I have not checked.

AI: Critical gaps: rent, experience, mortgage terms. By your own numbers: if opening consumes three months of five, about two remain, and the period in which the outlet must cover costs is shorter than people usually assume. This is arithmetic from your words; the assumption “opening costs three months” should be checked against an estimate, not a feeling. Before proposing options: what are you willing to check in the next 30 days without quitting?

You: I can on weekends and evenings.

Loop 2: expansion

AI: Then the choice is wider than “quit or stay”. C: 4–6 weeks of weekend work as a barista in an existing coffee shop — checks the experience question and shows real shifts. D: request written mortgage forbearance terms from the bank — checks the mortgage question, costs about an hour. E: pre-sale of subscriptions before signing a lease — checks demand. F: a smaller format: a counter or a point in someone else’s premises with low rent. For each option a key assumption: C — that weekend shifts are representative; D — that the bank will answer definitively; E — that payments will come not only from the close circle; F — that the format will not break the economics. […] The information most capable of changing the decision is the mortgage terms (D): if there is no forbearance, the path to self-sufficiency is tighter, and the check is almost free.

Loop 3: attack

You: I think E is best. Attack E first, then the “stay” option.

AI: Against E. Pre-sale among friends will measure your circle, not the neighborhood. Paying for a subscription to a non-existent outlet is different behavior from buying coffee every morning. A threshold of “30 subscriptions” without an audience requirement can be met and prove nothing. What must be true for the critique to be weak: buyers outside the circle, price close to the future one, a link to the neighborhood. Against “stay”. Inaction is not free, but it also does not exclude C, D, E: they can be run without quitting. Therefore “stay” for the duration of the tests is not a rejection of the idea but a data-collection mode. […] Pre-mortem (failure in 18 months), most plausible causes: rent above the expected share of revenue; understated load; savings ran out before break-even; the idea was appealing, daily work was not.

Loop 4: experiments and kill criteria

AI: I propose three tests over 30 days. D: written bank terms within 14 days. C: at least 4 shifts in a coffee shop. E: pre-sale, metric — payments from people outside the close circle, not responses and promises. Set the thresholds yourself before starting: I do not know your neighborhood and will not invent a number from thin air.

You: Threshold on E — 30 paid subscriptions from people outside the close circle in 30 days. If the bank refuses forbearance and payments are fewer than 15, I will not sign a lease and will revise the format or postpone for six months.

AI: The stop condition is measurable and tied to a date. I will add two things. Write down the forecast and confidence now, before the result: for example, “≥30 payments, 50%”. And put a review date in the calendar on day 30. Doubtful points: what counts as “close circle” — define in advance; and what to do at 20–29 payments, i.e. between thresholds. […]

5.3. What changed in the decision

The dialogue did not lead to an answer “open” or “don’t open”, and that is a normal result of the first cycle. The framing changed: instead of one fork with an irreversible step — a set of three cheap tests; two critical unknowns were revealed (mortgage terms and personal experience) that were not mentioned in the original question; thresholds and a stop condition were fixed; the decision to quit was deferred until data arrive.

7. Experiments and kill criteria

Bad question: “Do you think it will work?” Good question: “Which observation in the next 30 days is capable of substantially changing my decision?” Prioritize tests by three traits: high potential impact on the decision, high availability of the check, low cost of error of the test.

7.1. Experiment card

  • Hypothesis: what must turn out to be true?
  • Why critical: what happens to the decision if it is false?
  • Test: which observation will give the most direct and clean signal?
  • Metric: which concrete event or number counts as the result?
  • Deadline: when does the test end without exception?
  • Success threshold and stop threshold: at which values is the hypothesis confirmed, and at which do we stop?
  • Decision after the test: what changes in actions at each outcome, including the intermediate one?

A printable card template is in Appendix A.

7.2. When an experiment misleads

An experiment gives information only if it measures what is needed. Typical threats to validity:

ThreatExampleDefense
Biased sampleFriends and acquaintances buy the pre-saleCount payments outside the circle separately; define the circle in advance
Weak commitmentLikes and promises instead of paymentMetric — real money or an action that costs the customer
Different behaviorPrepaid subscription ≠ regular purchaseAdd a repeated signal: return customer, repeat payment
Small sizeThree payments as “confirmation of demand”Estimate in advance what size distinguishes the hypotheses
Threshold shiftingThreshold moves after the resultFix before the test; give a copy to a third party

7.3. Kill criteria

A stop condition must be measurable and fixed before the emotionally hard moment: “if X is not reached by date Y under conditions Z, we stop investing or radically change course”. This is the main protection against the sunk-cost trap — the tendency to continue a project because of what is already invested, not because of future return.

Three practices make kill criteria workable. First: formulate them in a calm state, when nothing has yet been invested. Second: show them to a third person who will remind you of them at the right moment. Third: write not only “when to stop” but also “what to do next”, otherwise stopping is perceived as defeat and is postponed.

8. Ready-made requests

8.1. Short request for a quick check

Help me break down a decision, but do not choose for me. First separate facts from my assumptions and ask questions without which the analysis will be unreliable. Then propose from three to five additional options to those I named, including a hybrid, a reversible one, and an option “first get the fact”. For each, state key assumptions, main risks, and the cheapest way to check. Separately note which claims need to be checked with external sources. If data are insufficient, say so plainly rather than guessing.

8.2. Full Master Prompt

How to apply. Do not send the entire text at once. Send the blocks “ROLE”, “DISCIPLINE”, “STOP RULE”, and “STAGE 1”, wait for the reply, then send the following stages one by one. The practical reason: in a long multi-stage prompt models often execute all stages in one answer even if asked to stop after the first. This is an empirical observation from practice, not a proven property of the architecture; if your model stops correctly, stages can be combined. The correspondence of stages to the method’s loops is shown in the table at the end of Section 4.

ROLE

You are my analytical partner on a complex decision. Do not choose for me and do not substitute my values with yours. Your task is to raise the quality of the process: make the unknown explicit, expand the space of options, attack assumptions, and propose checks.

DISCIPLINE

If my messages contain statements that I have already decided or of what I am sure, first rephrase them as neutral questions and answer the questions. The answer “insufficient data” is better than a confident guess; a confident error is worse than an acknowledged gap. Mark where you rely on general regularities and where on my data.

STOP RULE

First run only stage 1. After it, stop and wait for my answers. Do not move to options and recommendations until critical gaps are filled.

STAGE 1 — EPISTEMIC RADAR

Split the input into: confirmed facts; assumptions; interpretations; preferences and values; unknowns; claims requiring external verification. Ask only questions whose answers are capable of changing the decision. If there are no data, do not invent them.

STAGE 2 — KNOWLEDGE MAP

After my answers: what we know; what we do not know; which unknowns are critical; what can be obtained quickly; where independent verification is needed.

STAGE 3 — EXPAND THE SPACE

Do not limit yourself to A/B. Propose from three to five additional options, among which a hybrid or pilot, a reversible option (two-way door), and an option “first check, then decide” are mandatory. For each, state the key assumption and the exit cost.

STAGE 4 — RED TEAM

Attack each option symmetrically: 3–5 strongest arguments against; hidden assumptions; failure modes; what must be true for the critique to be weak. Do not soften critique for a pleasant tone.

STAGE 5 — PRE-MORTEM

Imagine that the chosen path failed in 12–24 months. Name 5–8 most plausible causes and separate the testable from speculation. At the end propose which 1–3 assumptions look critical; the choice of critical hypotheses remains with me.

STAGE 6 — EXPERIMENTS

For each critical hypothesis: hypothesis → test → metric → deadline → threshold → what changes in the decision. Seek high value of information at low cost. Propose thresholds as a question to me, not as a ready number, if you have no grounds.

STAGE 7 — REVERSIBILITY AND KILL CRITERIA

Assess the cost of error and the possibility of rollback. Propose measurable stop conditions and dates of review points. Do not use vague formulations such as “if it becomes clear that it is not working”.

STAGE 8 — FORECAST AND CALIBRATION

Help me formulate testable expectations for 30, 90, and 180 days: what exactly will count as having come true and by which observation. I record my own probability and confidence; do not substitute your numbers for mine. If there are no grounds for a number, say so.

STAGE 9 — FINAL SYNTHESIS

Do not choose for me. Give: 1) what we know; 2) what we do not know; 3) what is capable of changing the decision; 4) the next step with maximum information at minimum cost; 5) what requires external verification; 6) risks; 7) where you could be wrong or sensitive to wording.

FORMAT

Separate facts from conclusions. Do not present a scenario as a forecast. Do not present plausible text as proof.

9. How to run the work by the protocol

1. Pass triage (quick start, step 1) and fill the Decision Brief at least with rough drafts.

2. Give the model the initial data and ask it to run only the Epistemic Radar stage.

3. Answer the critical questions honestly. “I don’t know” is a legitimate and very useful answer.

4. Only after filling the gaps move on to expanding options.

5. Do not hide inconvenient facts and context from the model.

6. Conduct a symmetric red team and a pre-mortem.

7. Choose 1–3 critical hypotheses.

8. For each critical hypothesis, *before the test starts*, fix the forecast, confidence, thresholds, and kill criteria; give a copy to a third party.

9. Run quick experiments or check assumptions with independent authoritative sources.

10. Record your decision and its grounds: you take and record the decision, not AI.

11. After the result return to the journal, analyze the discrepancy with the forecast (Section 10.2), and update beliefs.

Independent repetition. For critically important decisions, run the analysis again in a new chat or with a different model, without copying previous conclusions. Strong divergence of results does not prove that one version is right; it signals that the decision is sensitive to wording or context, and that sensitivity is what needs to be examined: which assumptions led to different conclusions.

10. Decision journal and calibration

The journal protects against retrospective distortion and allows evaluating the quality of thinking at the moment of the decision, not by the outcome, which partly depends on chance.

DateHypothesisForecastConfidenceBasisCheckFactWhat I updated

01.10Pilot possible without quittingYes80%Personal time resource45 days……

If there is no objective base for a number, write “confidence low, insufficient data”. False precision is worse than an honest gap: a number creates a feeling of knowledge and makes later evaluation harder.

You record the forecast and confidence in this table. AI can propose wording and grounds, but numbers you are not prepared to stand by do not go into the journal: otherwise by the Brier score you will be calibrating the model, not yourself.

10.1. How to measure calibration

Calibration is the match between stated confidence and the real frequency of outcomes: events to which you assigned 70% should occur in roughly seven cases out of ten. A simple measure is the Brier score: the average square of the difference between the probability forecast and the outcome (1 if the event occurred, 0 if not). Lower is better.

Example on three forecasts: 70% (occurred), 60% (did not), 80% (occurred). Squared errors: 0.09; 0.36; 0.04. Mean: (0.09 + 0.36 + 0.04) ÷ 3 ≈ 0.16. For comparison, a constant forecast of “50%” always gives 0.25. Important caveat: on three forecasts the estimate is noisy; a meaningful conclusion is possible only after dozens of forecasts. The practice of systematic forecasting and belief revision is discussed in detail by Tetlock and Gardner.

10.2. How to analyze a mismatch between forecast and fact

When the fact diverged from the forecast, it is useful to name the type of discrepancy: it determines what to change in the next cycle. Write the type in the “What I updated” column.

TypeWhat it meansWhat to change
DataSource facts were wrong, incomplete, or outdatedSources and fact-checking (Loop 4); look earlier for an independent source
AssumptionThe assumption the plan rested on turned out false, and you did not check it or checked weaklyChoice of critical hypotheses (Loop 3) and design of the test (Section 7.2)
ReasoningData and assumptions were reasonable, but the conclusion from them was wrong: logic, bias, false precisionWay of reasoning: symmetric critique, ranges instead of exact numbers, second verification channel (Section 3.2)
ExecutionThe plan was correct but was executed differently or not executedRealism of the plan, load, review points; the idea need not necessarily be revised
ChanceThe outcome was determined by a circumstance that, given those data, could not reasonably have been foreseenUsually nothing in the method: record “chance” and leave the “What I updated” field empty or write “nothing to change in the method”

“Chance” is the last diagnosis, not the first: move to it when the previous four have been ruled out. A helpful question: if the same decision were taken with the same data many times, would the outcome be different often enough? If yes, part of the result is chance. That is why the journal evaluates the decision by the quality of the process, not only by the outcome.

11. Autonomy boundary and safety

Principle: the higher the cost of error, the harder facts are to verify with the external world, and the lower the reversibility of the decision, the less reason there is to give the system the right to act on its own.

SituationRecommended mode
Error is cheap and easily reversibleMore autonomy can be delegated to the system
Error is noticeable and easily checkableAutomation with regular audit
Error is costly but reversibleAI analyzes; final action with limits and human confirmation
Error is costly and hard to reverseIndependent verification and mandatory human decision
Choice is driven by valuesAI shows consequences but does not choose values for the human
High stakesAI analysis must not be the sole basis

Heightened caution is needed in medicine, law, taxes, large investments, and other domains with a high cost of error. Recheck current facts against independent sources. For agent systems, limit access, keep an action journal, and set mandatory human confirmation points.

Personal data. Do not enter into chats without necessity passwords, full bank card details, identity documents, and confidential information about third parties. Use the minimum necessary volume of data.

Human verification. If the cost of error is high, expert review should not be a formality. You need a domain specialist who can check the source facts and the applicability of conclusions to your situation.

Crisis support in companion software should use internationally recognized resources (for example the IASP directory of local resources; 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline in the US and Canada where applicable). Replace contacts for a specific country in configuration. The method text itself does not hard-code any single country’s helplines.

12. Applicability boundaries and typical failures of the method

12.1. When the method is needed and when it is overkill

The method costs time, so it should be applied selectively. It is warranted when at least two of four conditions hold: the error is costly; the step is hard to undo; there are substantial unknowns that can be resolved; the horizon of consequences is long. These same four questions form the triage at step 1 of “Quick start”.

  • Overkill if the decision is cheap and reversible: it is enough to try quickly. A full cycle here is harmful; it will consume more value than it brings.
  • Not applicable in pure form if the decision is determined only by values and there is nothing to discover: work with values is needed (conversation, reflection), not with facts.
  • Does not replace help if you are in acute crisis or severe distress: there you need a real person or a specialist, not a protocol. Decisions taken in such a state are better postponed if possible.

13. How to test the method itself

The method has not passed empirical verification, and the honest way to deal with that is to describe how to conduct it. Below is a scheme, not a result.

QuestionMeasureComment
Does the protocol surface more substantial unknowns?Number of critical unknowns before and after the protocol; rating by independent experts who do not know which condition they seeThe most direct and fastest check
Does it change decisions or only confidence?Share of decisions changed after the protocolDirection of change by itself is not a quality rating
Does it improve calibration?Brier score on a series of 30 or more forecasts against a baselineDiscipline of keeping the journal is required
Does it improve outcomes?A pre-fixed measure at 6–12 months: regret about the decision, objective indicatorsOutcomes are noisy and lagging
What is the cost?Time to decision, cases of analysis paralysisBenefit must exceed cost

Research design. Random assignment of comparable decisions or participants between free chat and the protocol; pre-registration of hypotheses and measures; blind rating by independent experts; publication of null results as well. Limitations of such work: self-selection of participants, heterogeneity of decisions, noisy delayed outcomes, observer effect. Until such data exist, the method should be treated as a reasonable hypothesis assembled from well-studied parts.

A reader can do a small version themselves: the journal from Section 10 already contains data for assessing calibration, and comparing the number of unknowns found before and after the protocol on several of one’s own decisions gives a preliminary, though not objective, signal.

14. Closing formula

Strong use of AI for decisions is not an attempt to get the “right answer” from a machine. It is designing a process in which the machine does what it does well, and the human retains control over what requires context, values, verification, and responsibility.

UNDERSTAND → EXPAND → ATTACK → VERIFY → LEARN

  • Start with triage: the method is not for every decision, but for costly, hard-to-reverse ones that contain resolvable unknowns.
  • Fluent model speech is not equal to truth; plausible text is not equal to proof.
  • Distinguish the model from the system around it; autonomy gives capabilities, not reliability automatically.
  • Do not call a scenario a forecast.
  • Phrase questions, not assertions; give facts fully, neutralize confidence.
  • Ask AI to attack your position and the position opposite to it.
  • If the choice seems binary, look for option C, but count the exit cost.
  • Check critical assumptions before irreversible steps; set kill criteria in advance.
  • Record your forecasts before the event and analyze discrepancies by type, including chance.
  • The more costly the error, the stronger human control must be.

Appendix D. Methodological caveat

  • On models. Not all models are built the same. Behavior depends on architecture, context, tools, system settings, and level of control. Studies of sycophancy and agent risks describe observed modes of behavior but do not mean that any model will behave that way in any situation.
  • On the method. Bifurcation Engine is a methodological framework, not a validated system. Its elements rest on separate research and practical approaches; the effectiveness of this exact composition has not been shown experimentally (see Section 13). Originality is claimed at the level of composition, not of individual techniques.
  • On examples. The examples given, including the dialogue and numeric calculations, are illustrative and are not proof of effectiveness.
  • On boundaries. The text does not replace consultation with specialists in domains with a high cost of error.
  • On sources. Conclusions about specific studies (Sections 3.1 and 3.2) are formulated from their abstracts and main results; transferability to personal decisions is not proven.
  • On localization. This edition is fully international: English language, USD (or generic units) in numeric examples, no country-specific helplines, currencies, or legal references hard-coded into the method. Companion software may ship configurable regional contacts; the method text does not.
