In-Situ Eutectic Ultra-High-Temperature Ceramic Coatings with Dispersed Rhenium Intermetallics for Reusable Thermal Protection Systems

1. Abstract

Reusable launch vehicles and hypersonic re-entry vehicles experience surface temperatures that routinely exceed 2000 °C on leading edges and nose tips. Conventional ultra-high-temperature ceramic (UHTC) coatings based on hafnium or zirconium diboride offer high melting points but remain vulnerable to thermal-shock cracking and delamination caused by coefficient-of-thermal-expansion mismatch with the underlying composite substrate.

This paper describes a coating architecture that forms its final microstructure in situ during the first high-temperature exposure. A precursor layer of HfB₂ or ZrB₂, SiC and finely dispersed rhenium-based intermetallic particles is applied to a carbon-carbon or ceramic-matrix-composite substrate. When the surface temperature reaches approximately 1800–1950 °C the constituents undergo a localized eutectic reaction that produces an interwoven multiphase network whose expansion behaviour more closely matches the substrate. The rhenium-rich particles act as crack-arrestors at grain boundaries. Simultaneously, controlled oxidation of the matrix generates a viscous hafnium-borosilicate glass that flows into surface microcracks and remains adherent under hypersonic shear. The result is a self-healing, thermally compliant coating intended for repeated re-entry cycles.

2. Background

Thermal-protection systems for reusable vehicles must survive multiple atmospheric entries without extensive refurbishment. At Mach numbers above 5 the sharpest radii encounter both extreme heat and atomic-oxygen attack. Monolithic diboride ceramics are refractory but brittle; rapid temperature changes open microcracks that allow oxygen to reach the substrate. Metallic interlayers intended to improve toughness introduce their own expansion mismatch and frequently delaminate.

A practical coating must therefore accommodate thermal strain, arrest cracks and continuously reseal the surface under oxidizing, high-shear conditions.

3. Related Work

HfB₂–SiC and ZrB₂–SiC coatings have been studied extensively. Self-healing behaviour arising from borosilicate or transition-metal-modified glasses is well documented, and additions such as TaSi₂, ZrSi₂ or MoSi₂ have been shown to improve glass viscosity and oxidation resistance above 2000 °C. In-situ formation of refractory oxide scales has also been reported. Rhenium and rhenium alloys are used in other high-temperature applications because of their melting point and ductility, yet their deliberate incorporation as discrete intermetallic clusters inside a UHTC matrix that forms an eutectic network on first heating is not described in the open literature examined by the author.

The present approach combines three elements: (1) an in-situ eutectic reaction that occurs during the first operational heat cycle, (2) dispersed rhenium-based particles that blunt or deflect cracks, and (3) a deliberately high-viscosity hafnium-borosilicate surface glass that resists aerodynamic stripping.

4. Architecture

Precursor composition

The coating is applied as a slurry or plasma-sprayed layer whose dry mass percentages lie in the following ranges:

HfB₂ or ZrB₂  65–75 %

SiC (particulates or short whiskers)  15–20 %

Rhenium-based intermetallic particles or Re-rich clusters  5–10 %

Minor sintering aids (B₄C or carbon)  1–3 %

Particle size of the rhenium-containing phase is kept below approximately 800 nm to ensure uniform distribution.

In-situ eutectic formation

No high-temperature densification step is required before flight. On the first exposure to temperatures above roughly 1800 °C the interfaces between the diboride, SiC and rhenium-rich particles undergo localized eutectic melting. On cooling, an interpenetrating multiphase microstructure forms whose effective coefficient of thermal expansion is closer to that of the carbon-carbon or SiC/SiC substrate than a purely ceramic layer.

Crack arrest

The rhenium-rich particles sit preferentially at grain boundaries. When a thermal-shock crack approaches, the higher local ductility and modulus of the rhenium phase blunt or deflect the crack tip, dissipating energy and limiting propagation.

Self-healing glass

Above 2000 °C in an oxidizing flow the surface reactions

2 SiC + 3 O₂ → 2 SiO₂ + 2 CO

HfB₂ + 2½ O₂ → HfO₂ + B₂O₃

produce a liquid that dissolves additional HfO₂. The resulting hafnium-borosilicate glass has substantially higher viscosity than pure silica or simple borosilicate. Capillary flow fills microcracks while the elevated viscosity keeps the glass from being stripped by the boundary-layer shear.

5. Scope and Limitations

The coating is intended for leading edges, nose tips, control surfaces and rocket-nozzle extensions of reusable launch and hypersonic vehicles. The substrate must be able to tolerate the brief excursion above 1800 °C needed to form the eutectic network (carbon-carbon or SiC-based CMCs). It is not suitable for low-melting metallic skins.

6. Implementation Notes

Organic binders used in slurry application must pyrolyse cleanly below 500 °C.

Rhenium-particle size distribution is controlled during milling or pre-alloying to avoid large agglomerates.

After the first flight or ground test, non-destructive inspection (ultrasonic or eddy-current) confirms that the eutectic conversion has occurred uniformly.

Coating thickness is typically 100–300 µm, adjusted for the expected heat flux and number of re-use cycles.
