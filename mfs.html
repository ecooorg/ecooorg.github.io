20 Multifunctional Solutions for Hydraulics and Structural Frameworks of Legged Robots: Self-Diagnostic Cartridge Valves, MRF and NiTi-Based Damping, Parametric Metamaterials, and Predictive Diagnostics

ZenodoTDCommons

Abstract

This document discloses twenty technical solutions for hydraulic drives, structural frameworks, and diagnostic systems of legged robots, unified by the design principle of a single physical element performing two or more functions simultaneously. These solutions encompass: self-diagnostic valves and seals utilizing impedance, piezoelectric, and NiTi mechanisms; damping and energy-recuperating structures based on magnetorheological fluids and liquid crystal elastomers; parametric metamaterial cells combining elastic, valving, and resonant functions; architectural designs for hardware-level hydraulic network survivability operating independently of electronics; cartridge interfaces with integrated opto-acoustic and electrochemical diagnostics; and a comprehensive structural joint architecture featuring three-level passive clamping and hydraulic lines routed internally through the load-bearing elements of a tensegrity framework.

Field of Technology and Purpose of the Publication

This disclosure relates to the field of robotics, specifically to hydraulic actuation systems, load-bearing frameworks, and diagnostic systems of legged and mobile robots, including cartridge-type (quick-disconnect) hydraulic modules, valves, damping elements, metamaterial structural frameworks, and embedded technical diagnostic tools.

Each of the solutions presented below is described in the following format: field of technology, problem and limitations of known solutions, core of the proposed solution, embodiments, and achieved technical effect. The formulations intentionally omit patent claims and commercial readiness assessments—the sole purpose of this document is the full disclosure of the technical substance of the solutions in a manner sufficient for a person skilled in the art.

1. Static Clamping and Sealing Mechanisms

1.1 Micro-leak sensor based on impedance monitoring of asymmetric Al₂O₃ membranes

Field of technology: Hydraulic valves and sealing connections of cartridge actuators in robotics; leak diagnostic devices.

Problem / limitations of known solutions: Known leak sensors require separate pressure/flow sensors, increasing the component count and points of failure; known membrane electroosmotic (EO) valves are inapplicable at actuator operating pressures (10–35 MPa), as EO pumps operate at pressures under 1 MPa.

Difference from known solutions: The same pair of electrodes that controls the EO pulse is utilized to register changes in impedance when sealing integrity is breached, without requiring a separate pressure or flow sensor.

Core of the solution: A pack of asymmetric Al₂O₃ membranes (pore size 20–30 nm) is applied as a precision micro-leak sensor in a low-load node—the cartridge interface joint (target pressure 0.5–1 MPa), where retention is provided by capillary forces and a short-duration EO pulse, while a breach of sealing integrity is detected by the change in electrical impedance of the membrane pack via the same electrodes that control the EO pulse—without additional sensors.

Embodiments:

  • The membrane pack is embedded into the cartridge sealing interface as a redundant monitoring loop for the integrity of the primary high-pressure seal.
  • Multiple independent membrane packs are distributed along the perimeter of the interface joint to localize the leak point.
  • The impedance signal is processed by a threshold comparator for a binary "leak/normal" indication without a microcontroller—a simplified implementation.
  • Ultrasonic self-cleaning of membrane pores as a routine maintenance operation during charging/docking.

Technical effect: Eliminates the need for separate pressure/flow sensors to monitor micro-leaks in a critical node; reduces component count and points of failure; the reduced target pressure makes the technology production-feasible at a significantly lower membrane cost.

1.2 Valve with a tungsten carbide sphere, zonally variable stiffness seat, and piezoelectric impact cycle counter

Field of technology: High-pressure valves of hydraulic actuators for the limbs of legged robots; embedded lifecycle accounting tools.

Problem / limitations of known solutions: Known methods for counting valve actuation cycles require separate sensors; triboelectric signal acquisition in a contact pair does not function in a conductive hydraulic fluid; a seat with full topological optimization is complex for mass production.

Difference from known solutions: Piezoelectric signal acquisition retains sensitivity in a conductive hydraulic fluid, where the known triboelectric method is non-functional.

Core of the solution: A WC sphere seats into a conical seat with three zones of variable stiffness, calculated for the impact spectrum of the limb; a thin piezoelectric (PZT) element under the seat generates an electrical impulse at each impact actuation of the closure element, enabling cycle counting and detection of contact surface degradation without additional sensors.

Embodiments:

  • The PZT element is integrated into the body of the seat directly beneath the contact zone.
  • An annular PZT element around the seat for signal acquisition from any point of the contact circumference.
  • A seat with three discrete stiffness zones fabricated within a single DMLS print.
  • DLC coating of the contact surface to reduce contamination risks and increase wear resistance.

Technical effect: Embedded valve lifecycle accounting and early detection of degradation without additional sensors; functionality in a real, conductive, and contaminated hydraulic environment; feasibility under mass DMLS production.

1.3 Thermohydraulic automatic safety valve based on a NiTiCu membrane with electrical override

Field of technology: Hardware-level (electronically independent) protective devices for hydraulic actuators against overheating; temperature-sensitive valves.

Problem / limitations of known solutions: The switching temperature accuracy (As) of binary nitinol (±10–20 °C) is insufficient for the narrow protective window between the operating and emergency fluid temperatures; a fully hardware-level automatic valve without the possibility of controller override can create new emergency situations when the "safe position" contextually depends on the robot's operating mode.

Difference from known solutions: Hardware protection against overheating operates autonomously in normal mode, but allows for controlled electrical blocking of actuation by the controller, which is absent in known purely thermomechanical automatic valves.

Core of the solution: A valve membrane made of a NiTiCu alloy (copper reduces the thermal hysteresis width to ±2 °C) switches directly via the temperature of the working fluid without electronics; the design is augmented with an electrical heating element, allowing the controller to forcibly block switching when required by the operational context.

Embodiments:

  • Base implementation—purely thermomechanical switching without electrical override.
  • Extended implementation with a heating element and a logic input from the controller for blocking/unblocking actuation.
  • Application of the same NiTiCu element as an isolating thermal gate in an inter-circuit bridge of a hydraulic network (see section 4.1).

Technical effect: Autonomous hardware-level protection against overheating without controller intervention in normal mode, combined with contextual controllability via an optional electrical override; the enhanced accuracy of the alloy reduces the risk of false positives.

1.4 Self-learning manufacturing pipeline for verifying zero-leakage of a precision poppet

Field of technology: Manufacturing of precision hydraulic seals by additive methods (DMLS); quality control of the seal.

Problem / limitations of known solutions: Analytical FEM models of contact friction and deformation in hydraulic seals yield an error of 15–30%, which prevents guaranteeing zero leakage based solely on simulation without physical verification.

Difference from known solutions: The contact friction model is refined using measured actual leakage data after each production batch, rather than remaining unchanged as in known purely simulation-based approaches.

Core of the solution: A closed-loop manufacturing cycle: a calculated conical poppet geometry, topologically optimized and verified by CFD simulation under a variance of manufacturing tolerances, is augmented with a stage for measuring actual leakage after printing a batch of products; the measurement results are automatically used to correct the contact friction model before the next iteration.

Embodiments:

  • Model correction is performed after each production batch (batch-level feedback).
  • Model correction is performed for each product individually during 100% inspection (unit-level feedback) for small-scale or critical production.
  • A library of refined friction models is stored centrally and applied to new seal geometries on the same production line.

Technical effect: Systematic reduction of leakage prediction error with each manufactured batch; attainment of near-zero leakage without manual finishing of the seal.

2. Variable Stiffness, Energy Recuperation, and "Mechanical Intelligence"

2.1 MRF-damping element of dual mode (stride energy resonator / protective impact damper)

Field of technology: Damping and energy-recuperating structures of robot limbs based on magnetorheological fluids (MRF).

Problem / limitations of known solutions: MRF degrades through multiple mechanisms simultaneously (particle sedimentation, oxidation, abrasive wear) with a service life on the order of 200–500 hours of intensive operation; large-scale (thoracic) implementation requires the simultaneous development of the MRF formulation, design, and control system, which sharply increases development costs and timelines.

Difference from known solutions: The same volume of MRF performs the function of a resonant stride energy accumulator and a protective impact damper, whereas known designs utilize separate nodes for these functions.

Core of the solution: A metamaterial structure filled with MRF operates in two modes through the very same physical fluid volume: in normal walking mode—as a resonant accumulator of kinetic stride energy (low MRF viscosity, tuning to the stride frequency);

upon detecting an impact or fall—as a maximum damper (the MRF solidifies in <5 ms as current in the coils rises). Mode switching is based on the amplitude of input acceleration.

Embodiments:

  • Pilot implementation: a single MRF damper in the ankle joint—minimal MRF volume, simple fluid replacement during maintenance.
  • Scaled implementation: a distributed thoracic structure composed of multiple MRF cavities after verifying the principle on a joint.
  • Induction pre-heating of the MRF to prevent thickening in sub-zero temperatures.
  • Generatively designed maze-partitions inside the cavity to tune the resonant frequency to a specific platform.

Technical effect: A single physical volume performs the functions of energy recuperation and protective damping during accidents without doubling the mass and volume of the structure; step-by-step implementation reduces the principle verification cost by two orders of magnitude.

2.2 Self-diagnostic structural framework: thermochromic PVD coating of TBS cells and acoustic emission of internal channels.

Field of technology: Load-bearing structures of robots based on bistable metamaterial cells; tools for visual and acoustic diagnostics of structural fatigue.

Problem / limitations of known solutions: Introducing chromogenic dyes directly into metallic DMLS powder contaminates the powder bed and renders mass production economically unviable; bulk chromogenic effects in metal have only been implemented in laboratory conditions in practice.

Difference from known solutions: Visual stress indication is implemented by a surface PVD coating compatible with serial DMLS equipment, rather than bulk alloying of the powder, which is applicable only in laboratory conditions.

Core of the solution: Bistable gyroid cells (TBS-Cell), grown in the walls of hydraulic channels and replacing discrete valves and springs, receive a thin-film thermochromic PVD coating with a phase transition (e.g., VO₂) on their external surfaces, which changes color when a critical temperature/stress is exceeded; internal channels inaccessible to visual inspection are additionally equipped with acoustic emission sensors.

Embodiments:

  • The PVD coating is applied to all external load-bearing surfaces of the structure.
  • The coating is localized only in zones predicted by generative calculation as stress concentration areas.
  • Acoustic sensors are placed discretely at key nodes of the internal channels instead of a continuous coating.

Technical effect: A visual stress map of the external surface during routine inspection without disassembly or instruments; monitoring of internal channels using an established non-destructive testing (NDT) method; manufacturing complexity is reduced by an order of magnitude compared to bulk chromogenic alloying.

2.3 Single 4-parametric metamaterial cell (t, L, e, θ) as a platform mechanics generator

Field of technology: Generative design of load-bearing and functional (valving, resonant) elements from a single metamaterial building block for additive manufacturing.

Problem / limitations of known solutions: Gyroid cells in DMLS exhibit anisotropy of properties depending on the print orientation relative to the load; a parametric model that neglects this orientation yields systematically incorrect stiffness predictions; sharp parameter transitions between adjacent zones create stress concentrators at the boundary.

Difference from known solutions: The cell orientation parameter accounts for the anisotropy of properties during DMLS printing, which known models of gyroid cells with fewer parameters do not reflect.

Core of the solution: A single cell with four parameters—wall thickness t, lattice period L, eccentricity e, and cell orientation θ relative to the load axis—allows a single mathematical description to yield soft elastic, rigid load-bearing, bistable valving, and resonant zones in different areas of a part; the transition between zones with different parameters is executed via a transitional zone of gradient parameter variation over a length of 2–3 lattice periods.

Embodiments:

  • The 4-parametric model is used for the complete automatic generation of the entire platform mechanics from a single pipeline.
  • The parametric library is applied locally—only for individual functional nodes within a traditionally designed structure.
  • A library of fatigue characteristics for combinations of (t, L, e, θ) is published and saved as a reusable dataset for subsequent projects.

Technical effect: Complete unification of manufacturing (one material, one pipeline, one post-processing) with physically accurate stiffness prediction and elimination of stress concentrators at zone boundaries; the cell forms a universal building block for legged robot mechanics.

2.4 LCE-based joint clamp with induction heating (RoHS-compliant In-Bi alloy) and optional strain gauging

Field of technology: Joint clamping actuators based on liquid crystal elastomers (LCE) with low-melting conductive alloys; embedded strain gauging.

Problem / limitations of known solutions: Field's metal applied in known solutions contains cadmium and lead, which are prohibited by RoHS standards for serial products; the simultaneous development of the material, technology, and verification of three functions at once (actuation, heating, strain gauging) substantially increases development costs and risks.

Difference from known solutions: The conductive filler is a eutectic In-Bi alloy without cadmium and lead, whereas known solutions utilize toxic Field's metal, which is banned by RoHS standards.

Core of the solution: An LCE composite with micro-inclusions of a low-melting conductive alloy is used as a joint clamp: induction heating of the micro-inclusions accelerates thermal switching of the matrix (less than 1 s instead of slow passive heating), and the change in electrical resistance of the same inclusions under deformation serves as a strain gauge signal.

Embodiments:

  • Generation 1 (RoHS-compliant): the conductive filler is a eutectic In-Bi alloy (Tm ≈ 72 °C);
    heating and clamping functions are implemented.
  • Generation 2: a strain-gauging function is added to Generation 1 via the same inclusions.
  • The topology of the inclusions is designed for uniform distribution of heating and strain signals throughout the volume of the material.

Technical effect: Eliminates toxic material as a barrier to commercialization; step-by-step implementation yields an intermediate commercial product (2 functions) earlier than a full three-functional solution and reduces the aggregate development risk.

2.5 LCE muscle with a stepped non-linear stiffness profile and proximal transmission via a pre-tensioned nitinol cable

Field of technology: Soft actuators (artificial muscles) based on liquid crystal elastomers for grippers and adaptive supports.

Problem / limitations of known solutions: A continuous gradient of mesogen orientation along the fiber during 4D printing is poorly reproducible between batches (stiffness profile variance of 20–30%); cable transmission from a proximally located actuator to a distal gripper introduces backlash and elastic deformation, reducing the non-linear effect engineered into the material.

Difference from known solutions: A stepped orientation profile of mesogens composed of 2–3 segments is more reproducible between batches compared to the known continuous gradient.

Core of the solution: An LCE fiber with a stepped (2–3 segments) mesogen orientation profile provides a soft start of the stroke (delicate contact) and a sharp increase in stiffness at the end of the stroke (forceful retention) without mechanical stops; the actuator is placed proximally, and force is transmitted by a pre-tensioned nitinol cable with a known elastic characteristic accounted for in the control model.

Embodiments:

  • Two-segment profile (soft/rigid)—minimal manufacturing complexity.
  • Three-segment profile for a smoother transition between modes.
  • Application in a surgical instrument where the repeatability of delicate contact is critical.

Technical effect: Significantly higher reproducibility of the stiffness profile between batches compared to a continuous gradient; predictable summation of muscle non-linearity and linear transmission elasticity in the control model.

3. Damping of Hydraulic Shock and Transient Processes

3.1 Passive fluidic diode hydraulic shock damper without moving parts, verified at realistic Reynolds numbers

Field of technology: Passive damping of hydraulic shock in hydraulic cartridge valves without moving parts.

Problem / limitations of known solutions: The fluidic diode principle has been mastered for microfluidics at Reynolds numbers Re < 100, whereas in robot hydraulics Re = 10³–10⁵, where resistance asymmetry can sharply drop due to turbulence; the surface roughness of DMLS channels (Ra 10–20 µm) distorts the calculated geometry, and post-processing alters geometry unpredictably.

Difference from known solutions: The maze geometry is verified by CFD calculation directly at the Reynolds numbers of robot hydraulics (10³–10⁵), rather than being transferred without modification from microfluidic solutions calculated for Re < 100.

Core of the solution: An asymmetric-flow DMLS maze inside the cartridge provides minimal resistance in the valve opening direction and non-linearly growing resistance in the closing direction, automatically damping the hydraulic shock pressure peak (up to ≤12 MPa) without moving parts or electronics; the geometry is designed and verified by CFD calculation at the real Reynolds numbers of the platform, with DMLS roughness incorporated as a simulation parameter, and confirmed on an experimental test rig prior to production.

Embodiments:

  • The maze is designed for a specific load spectrum of the platform (generative design).
  • A universal maze with averaged characteristics for several cartridge sizes.
  • Electrochemical, abrasive-extrusion, or chemical post-processing of internal maze channels is applied purposefully only in zones critical to pressure drop, reducing DMLS print roughness to Ra 1.6–2.5 µm to prevent premature cavitational erosion of walls at peak Reynolds numbers.

Technical effect: Passive, electronically independent, and adjustment-free damping of hydraulic shock; feasibility on existing DMLS equipment subject to mandatory CFD verification and bench testing in the real Re range.

3.2 Paired lifecycle counter using Belleville washers of different materials to separately indicate impact and thermal history

Field of technology: Mechanical (electronically independent) indicators of accumulated lifecycle for valve nodes.

Problem / limitations of known solutions: The residual deformation of a single Belleville washer depends simultaneously on the number and energy of impacts, temperature, and corrosion, which prevents isolating the contribution of each factor from a single measurement.

Difference from known solutions: A pair of washers made of different materials separates the contributions of impact and thermal degradation based on the difference in their deformations, whereas a known single washer yields only a combined, inseparable metric.

Core of the solution: A pair of precision Belleville washers made of different materials is installed beneath the valve seat—one with known sensitivity predominantly to impact fatigue, the other predominantly to thermal deformation; the difference in their measurable residual deformation (measured with a gauge in seconds during routine inspection) separates the contribution of impact and thermal lifecycles.

Embodiments:

  • The washers are installed coaxially behind one another under a single seat.
  • The washers are installed in parallel, identically loaded nodes for independent comparison.
  • A calibration table "deformation → remaining lifecycle" is published as part of the maintenance regulations.

Technical effect: A simple, completely hardware-free method for separate evaluation of the impact and thermal lifecycle of a cartridge during routine inspection.

4. Architecture, Survivability, and Manufacturing

4.1 Decentralized hydraulic network with hardware-level pressure isolation of a damaged circuit

Field of technology: Survivability architecture of a decentralized hydraulic network for robot limbs; hardware-level (electronically independent) means of damage localization.

Problem / limitations of known solutions: Fluid transfer from an intact circuit to a damaged one via a trigger that actuates based on fluid overheating in the damaged circuit physically exacerbates fluid loss through the breach site and exhibits an actuation delay of tens of seconds.

Difference from known solutions: The damaged circuit is isolated by pressure in less than 100 ms, whereas known solutions based on a thermal trigger transfer fluid to the breach site with a delay of tens of seconds.

Core of the solution: The hydraulic network is divided into isolated circuits (for example, four circuits of ~240 ml volume) connected by membrane mechanical actuators or NiTi thermal gates; when pressure in the damaged circuit drops below a threshold, the membrane mechanically closes the gate, isolating the damaged circuit from the rest of the network in less than 100 ms, completely at the hardware level, without electronics or controller intervention.

Embodiments:

  • Purely mechanical membrane trigger based on pressure—the base implementation with the highest responsiveness.
  • A NiTi thermal gate of the design according to section 1.3, applied as an isolating (non-transferring) element.
  • Combined implementation: a membrane trigger as the primary rapid mechanism, and a NiTi gate as a secondary backup.

Technical effect: Localization of circuit damage in less than 100 ms without electronics; preservation of network functionality even in the event of a control electronics failure.

4.2 DMLS housing with segregated cavities for hydraulic and high-voltage lines separated by a parylene-insulated partition

Field of technology: Additive manufacturing of combined hydraulic and electrical (high-voltage) lines in a single robot housing.

Problem / limitations of known solutions: Fully monolithic multi-material printing of metal-dielectric remains at the level of isolated laboratory samples; adhesion between the metallic matrix and dielectric inserts during thermal cycling is unresolved; the uniformity of parylene-CVD coating in winding channels of small diameter cannot be verified without destroying the part; insulation breakdown requires replacing the entire block.

Difference from known solutions: Hydraulic channels and high-voltage lines are separated by a mechanical partition within a single DMLS part, eliminating the need to resolve the metal-dielectric adhesion problem characteristic of known fully monolithic multi-material printing.

Core of the solution: Hydraulic channels and high-voltage lines are placed in parallel cavities of a single DMLS part, separated by a shared mechanical partition; parylene-CVD insulation is applied only to the partition, rather than across the entire path of the channel.

Embodiments:

  • The partition is executed as a solid wall of constant thickness.
  • A partition of variable thickness, optimized for the local electric field gradient.
  • The partition is executed as a removable/replaceable section for partial repair without replacing the entire block.

Technical effect: Retains the primary advantage of the concept (reducing the number of interfaces and failure points due to a single housing) at a manufacturing complexity an order of magnitude lower than that of fully monolithic multi-material printing; partial maintainability.

4.3 Pair of separate piezoelectric elements for simultaneous ultrasonic cleaning and through-transmission acoustic analysis of the working fluid

Field of technology: Embedded ultrasonic cleaning and acoustic diagnostics of working fluid quality in a hydraulic cartridge.

Problem / limitations of known solutions: A single piezoelectric element switched between emission and reception modes requires impedance commutation with a transient process; in contaminated fluid containing air bubbles, the acoustic signal scatters and attenuates heavily, reducing sensitivity compared to laboratory conditions.

Difference from known solutions: Two dedicated piezoelectric elements on opposite walls of the channel eliminate the need for impedance commutation and ensure through-transmission of the signal, which is unavailable with a known single switchable element.

Core of the solution: Two piezoelectric elements on opposite walls of a channel—one operates continuously in emission mode (ultrasonic cleaning of channels via cavitation bubbles), the other continuously in reception mode, analyzing the through-transmission acoustic spectrum of fluid scattering/absorption to detect particles, viscosity changes, and the onset of cavitation.

Embodiments:

  • The elements are installed on opposite walls of a straight section of the channel.
  • Multiple pairs of elements along different sections of the cartridge to localize the contamination zone.
  • Emission and reception modes cyclically alternate between the pair of elements to obtain a differential signal.

Technical effect: Eliminates the impedance commutation problem; doubled sensitivity due to through-transmission of the signal in a real, opaque oil medium; predictive maintenance prior to component failure.

5. Interfaces, Maintainability, and Diagnostics

5.1 Quick-disconnect cartridge interface with a magnetofluidic (ferrofluidic) port seal

Field of technology: Quick-disconnect hydraulic interfaces of cartridges resistant to contamination during field replacement.

Problem / limitations of known solutions: Mechanical (spring or NiTi-driven) shutters introduce additional moving parts into a loaded interface; during replacement in muddy environments, particles can enter the shutter mechanism and jam it.

Difference from known solutions: Port sealing is accomplished by a ferrofluid without moving mechanical parts, excluding the risk of jamming by contaminant particles characteristic of known mechanical shutters.

Core of the solution: In the disconnected state, the hydraulic ports of the cartridge are hermetically sealed by a ferrofluid held by a permanent magnet above the port—without moving mechanical parts; upon mating, the fluid pressure of the cartridge displaces the ferrofluid, opening the passage.

Embodiments:

  • A ferrofluid seal on each cartridge port individually.
  • A shared ferrofluid barrier along the perimeter of the entire mating interface.
  • Combination with a sloped interface surface and micro-grooves that divert mechanical dirt particles away from the active contact zone.

Technical effect: Zero risk of mechanism jamming by contaminant particles while maintaining a strict target schedule of hot-swapping the cartridge in ≤90 seconds under field conditions.

5.2 Diagnostic cartridge with segregated sensor frontend (replaceable section) and edge-AI model (permanent module)

Field of technology: Embedded predictive diagnostics of working fluid and cartridge condition based on edge-AI.

Problem / limitations of known solutions: Placing an edge-AI chip directly in a replaceable cartridge leads to the loss of the accumulated diagnostic model with each replacement; an optical analyzer and an AI chip significantly raise the cost of a consumable item.

Difference from known solutions: The edge-AI diagnostic model resides in a permanent module of the robot rather than in the replaceable cartridge, excluding the loss of accumulated data during each consumable replacement.

Core of the solution: The architecture is split into permanent and replaceable parts: the edge-AI model for predictive diagnostics permanently resides in a non-replaceable module of the robot, while the replaceable cartridge contains only the sensor frontend—a miniature optical fluid condition analyzer (LED + photodiode) and a high-frequency piezo sensor for wall vibration; frontend signals are transmitted to the permanent AI module for processing.

Embodiments:

  • A permanent AI module serves multiple cartridges simultaneously (for multi-limb platforms).
  • A light version—only the optical frontend without the piezo sensor for less critical nodes.
  • A wireless signal transmission interface for frontend→AI to simplify mating mechanics.

Technical effect: The accumulated diagnostic model is preserved through any cartridge replacement; the cost of the consumable product is reduced by moving the expensive AI chip to the permanent framework.

5.3 Continuous wedge-type emergency clamp with strain-gauge feedback on actual clamping force

Field of technology: Emergency friction clamps for the joints of legged robots with feedback.

Problem / limitations of known solutions: Discrete (stepped) fail-safe clamps generate an impact pulse upon actuation; known continuous self-locking wedge clamps are purely passive devices without the capability for controller-regulated dosing of the engagement degree.

Difference from known solutions: An embedded strain sensor transmits a signal regarding the actual clamping force to the controller, enabling modulated dosing of the engagement degree, which is absent in known passive wedge clamps.

Core of the solution: A wedge mechanism with continuous (stepless) self-locking engagement at any point of the joint stroke (backlash ≤0.5°), whose wedge geometry is optimized for a minimal impact pulse, is augmented with an embedded strain sensor in the contact zone that measures the actual clamping force and transmits the signal to the controller, which can dose the degree of engagement.

Correlation with section 6.1: The same principle of self-locking wedge geometry (tan α < μ) is applied in section 6.1 for a different task—there, the wedge step is calculated for permanent, routine holding of a static load and is not equipped with a strain sensor for active dosing by a controller; the present solution remains a controllable emergency brake with feedback on the actual clamping force.

Embodiments:

  • The strain sensor is embedded directly into the wedge.
  • The strain sensor is embedded into the mating part (seat) of the engagement.
  • The strain sensor signal is also used by the controller to diagnose wear on the contact surface of the wedge over time.

Technical effect: Smooth rather than stepped emergency stopping with a minimal impact pulse; controllable dosing of the clamping degree by the controller turns a passive clamp into an active element with feedback.

5.4 Three-barrier galvanic corrosion protection with redundant galvanic and capacitive breakdown monitoring

Field of technology: Galvanic corrosion protection in hybrid metal structures (dissimilar metals in contact); embedded means for early detection of insulation breakdown.

Problem / limitations of known solutions: Monitoring barrier system breakdown via galvanic current measurement functions only when an electrolyte (moisture) is present in the contact zone; in a dry environment, the signal is absent even during physical insulation breach.

Difference from known solutions: The capacitive channel detects breakdown of the dielectric barrier independently of environmental moisture, whereas the known galvanic method requires the presence of an electrolyte.

Core of the solution: Three physical barriers (laser plasma nitriding of contact surfaces, a PTFE washer, an anaerobic dielectric sealant) are augmented with two independent integrity monitoring channels: a galvanic microammeter (functional when moisture is present) and a capacitive method that detects changes in capacitance between dissimilar metals upon dielectric layer breakdown, independently of environmental moisture.

Embodiments:

  • Both monitoring channels operate continuously and in parallel.
  • The capacitive channel is primary, and the galvanic channel provides additional confirmation when moisture is present.
  • Application of the three-barrier system with dual detection as a replicated node for any hybrid metal structures of the robot.

Technical effect: Early detection of barrier protection breach prior to the appearance of visible corrosion, independently of environmental moisture, without disassembling the node.

5.5 Hybrid (predictive + hardware backup) proactive telemetry protection against impulsive EM noise from GaN drivers

Field of technology: Protection of telemetry sensors against electromagnetic interference from power GaN drivers in robotic systems.

Problem / limitations of known solutions: A purely predictive model trained on a deterministic pattern of GaN pulses in normal mode is unreliable during transient processes (start-up, load changes, emergency shutdown), where the pattern is unpredictable and interference intensity is maximal.

Difference from known solutions: A hardware backup channel with a 100 ns delay covers transient regimes where a known purely predictive model is unreliable due to the unpredictability of the noise pattern.

Core of the solution: A hybrid protection scheme: primary mode—an edge-AI model trained on the temporal pulse pattern of GaN drivers opens a sensor blanking window 10–50 µs before the expected pulse (proactive protection); backup mode—a fast hardware comparator with a 100 ns delay switches the sensors into a blanking mode upon direct detection of a pulse edge, covering unpredictable transient processes.

Embodiments:

  • A single edge-AI model serves all GaN channels of the platform without algorithm modification during scaling.
  • A separate AI model for each power channel for platforms with heterogeneous drives.
  • The hardware comparator is implemented as a separate IC; a logical OR of the predictive and reactive signals forms the final blanking window.

Technical effect: Improvement of telemetry quality in a noisy power environment both in normal mode (proactive protection without transient artifacts) and in transient mode (backup hardware protection); reliability across all operating regimes.

6. Integrated Structural Joint Architecture

6.1 Fluid-mediated structural joint architecture with three-level passive clamping and integration of hydraulic lines into a tensegrity framework

Field of technology: Load-bearing joint modules of anthropomorphic and legged robots; hardware-level (passive) emergency load clamping systems; architecture of hydraulic lines within structural frameworks.

Problem / limitations of known solutions: Electric joint drives maintain a static load only under continuous power consumption (hundreds to thousands of watts), which sharply reduces autonomy during prolonged posture holding; electrohydraulic equivalents hold the load only partially via valves and require block disassembly during maintenance; known passive clamps typically implement only a single stage of protection and do not segregate clamping and damping functions across independent kinematic axes.

Difference from known solutions: Three levels of passive clamping actuate across independent kinematic axes without electronics, and hydraulic lines pass internally through the very load-bearing struts of the tensegrity framework, which is not found in known electric and electrohydraulic joint architectures that require continuous power consumption to hold a load.

Core of the solution: The joint module integrates three kinematically decoupled, completely passive clamping levels that actuate sequentially without electronics or controller intervention: (1) a primary self-locking wedge gate with a 4.5° wedging angle operating under the condition tan α < μ, featuring laser-textured micro-grooves on the contact surfaces that maintain a friction coefficient of no less than 0.11 (nominal) / 0.095 (conservative limit of resource depletion) within a colloidal magnetic fluid medium; (2) a secondary loop of a granulated medium transitioning from a pseudo-liquid to a locked solid state when local pressure drops below 3.0 MPa, providing a margin of about 0.9 MPa against operational pressure drops down to 2.1 MPa during sharp maneuvers; the stable resistance of the granulate to abrasion under severe vibration of a legged robot will require selecting high-strength ceramic or carbide microspheres to avoid turning the medium into powder and jamming the joint; (3) a tertiary radial frictional stop, kinematically decoupled from the first two stages, limiting backlash to ≤0.5°. All three stages are integrated into a single replaceable cartridge. The structural struts of the tensegrity framework are made hollow and serve as channels for the high-pressure hydraulic line, which physically separates the pressurized hydraulic circuit and the dry, tensioned network of the framework without additional external piping.

Correlation with section 5.3: The primary stage utilizes the same principle of self-locking wedge geometry (tan α < μ) as section 5.3, but here the wedge is calculated for permanent, routine holding of a static load rather than a controlled emergency stop, and it does not contain a strain sensor for dosing by a controller—this function is not required in this architecture since controlled braking during an abnormal situation is provided by a separate, kinematically independent second stage (granulated self-locking medium).

Embodiments:

  • Base implementation of the cartridge without a damping module.
  • Implementation with an additional hydro-mechanical damping module (a micro-throttle maze with Belleville springs) that limits hydraulic shock and maintains the valve closing time within a narrow temporal window.
  • Specialized implementations of the cartridge housing and seals for ionizing radiation environments (titanium housing, radiation-resistant nitrile seals), for deep-sea conditions (pneumatic purging of mating surfaces, pressure compensation of the magnetic fluid), and for abrasive dust conditions (open laser micro-grooves for self-cleaning of contact surfaces, ultrasonic unlocking of the granulated medium).
  • Embedded inductive telemetry of the contact surfaces and the granulated medium with a sampling frequency of no less than 500 Hz for diagnosing resource depletion.

Technical effect: Static load holding occurs without power expenditure on the mechanical lock itself—energy consumption is limited to telemetry; the cartridge architecture allows for field replacement of the node using standard tools; distributing functions among three independent passive stages eliminates simultaneous failure due to a common cause; routing hydraulic lines inside the structural load-bearing elements of the tensegrity framework eliminates separate pressure piping and associated points of failure.

Cross-Cutting Principle of the Disclosed Solutions

Common to all of the presented solutions is the principle of performing at least two functions through a single physical element or a single physical volume—simultaneously reducing the number of components, mass, and points of failure. The disclosed embodiments deliberately span both basic and extended implementations of each solution, in order to establish, as prior art, the widest possible range of specific engineering realizations of the general principle.

This publication does not limit the described solutions to the embodiments presented: a person skilled in the art may combine elements from different solutions (for example, applying the node of section 1.3 as an element of section 4.1) and adapt the numerical parameters to a specific platform without departing from the disclosed technical substance.

Related Prior Art Landscape (Non-Exhaustive)

This disclosure intentionally omits citations, consistent with common defensive-publication practice. The following non-exhaustive list identifies independently verifiable prior art closest to five of the solutions above, to aid searchability and to make explicit how each solution differs from the nearest identified analog. It does not constitute a complete prior art search and is not extended to all twenty solutions.

  • Section 1.3 (NiTiCu thermal safety valve with electrical override): thermally actuated shape-memory-alloy valves with heater-assisted override are known, e.g. US5584432A (anti-scald valve with an SMA actuator) and US8499779B2 (nitinol valve with an integrated heater for controller-initiated reset). The distinguishing element of this solution is the NiTiCu alloy choice, which narrows the switching hysteresis to ±2°C (versus ±10–20°C for binary nitinol), rather than the general concept of heater-assisted override, which already exists in the cited art.
  • Section 2.1 (dual-mode MRF damping element: stride-energy resonator / impact damper): magnetorheological damping combined with energy recovery in a limb context is described in US7485152B2 (prosthetic knee with MR damping and regenerative braking) and US7335233B2 (MR fluid compositions for prosthetic knees), and surveyed in Dutra et al., “Magnetorheological fluid in prostheses: A state-of-the-art review,” 2024. The distinguishing element of this solution is the use of a single MRF volume for both functions via amplitude-triggered mode switching, rather than separate damping and energy-recovery subsystems as in the cited prosthetics art.
  • Section 3.1 (passive fluidic-diode hydraulic shock damper without moving parts): the no-moving-part fluidic diode principle originates with N. Tesla, “Valvular conduit,” U.S. Patent 1,329,559 (1920), and was extended to microfluidic Reynolds numbers (Re < 100) in Groisman & Quake, “A microfluidic rectifier,” Phys. Rev. Lett. 92, 094501 (2004). As already stated in the solution itself, the distinguishing element is CFD verification and DMLS-roughness modeling directly at robotic-hydraulics Reynolds numbers (Re = 10³–10⁵), a regime not covered by the cited microfluidic work.
  • Section 5.1 (ferrofluidic port seal for a quick-disconnect cartridge): ferrofluidic seals for rotating shafts are foundational and commercialized at scale, originating with R. Rosensweig, U.S. Patent 3,620,584 (1971), and offered commercially as, e.g., Ferrotec Ferrofluidic® seals. The distinguishing element of this solution is its application to a static docking-port seal displaced by fluid pressure on mating, rather than to a continuously rotating shaft, which is the standard use case in the cited art.
  • Section 6.1 (tensegrity joint architecture with internal hydraulic lines and three-level passive locking): tensegrity robots with functional struts are documented in NASA’s SUPERball / ReCTeR platforms (Sabelhaus et al., “System design and locomotion of SUPERball, an untethered tensegrity robot,” 2015) and in Spikebot (pneumatically actuated linear struts, 2024). In the cited platforms, the struts themselves serve as the actuation or cable-routing channel for locomotion; the distinguishing element of this solution is routing a separate pressurized hydraulic supply through the struts to serve an independent, passive, multi-stage joint-locking mechanism decoupled from strut actuation.
