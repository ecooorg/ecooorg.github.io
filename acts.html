Autonomous Cryogenic Two-Stage In-Loop Sealing of Microdefects Using Circulating Nanocapsulated Agents (ACTIS)

1. Abstract

Operation of large-scale cryogenic installations—particle accelerators, fusion reactors, space propulsion stages and quantum systems—demands continuous maintenance of leak-tightness in coolant and liquefied-gas circulation loops. Conventional methods for locating and repairing microleaks through intergranular pores of structural materials and microcracks in welded joints require complete shutdown of equipment, prolonged thermal recovery of isolated sectors and external mechanical intervention.

This publication presents a method of Autonomous Cryogenic Two-Stage In-Loop Sealing (ACTIS) in which the working cryogenic fluid itself serves as the carrier medium for functional nanocapsules. The ACTIS method relies on thermodynamic, mechanical, and electro-inductive triggers operating in a dual-stage sequence. Upon entering a microdefect, the vitrified nanocapsules undergo localized geometric confinement and steric jamming driven by hydrodynamic pressure. This forms a temporary solid-state restriction that anchors the agent within the fissure, enabling subsequent thermal activation and polymerisation without cycle interruption.

Order-of-magnitude estimates, drawn from scaling considerations and available literature data, indicate that a typical sub-micron to micron-scale defect (width 0.1–10 µm, length 10–100 µm) requires a sealing-agent volume of 10⁻¹⁵–10⁻¹² L. At capsule concentrations not exceeding 0.05 wt% and volume fractions φ ≈ 10⁻⁴–10⁻³, filling is expected to be achieved within the flow transit time (milliseconds to seconds) by means of a hybrid mechanism. The influence of the particles on the properties of superfluid helium-II at these concentrations is assessed as negligible on the basis of existing dispersion studies, though this remains subject to direct experimental verification.

A differentiation matrix of technological solutions is proposed, separating the architecture of agents and auxiliary equipment into two independent categories: direct integration into existing systems without modification of their geometry, and incorporation into newly designed installations.

The document records the conceptual architecture, protective mechanisms, agent modifications, order-of-magnitude quantitative estimates and the invention formula at the stage of scientific exploration and invention—prior to prototyping and laboratory experiments. The publication establishes worldwide priority (prior art).

2. Background and Overcoming of Critical Vulnerabilities

Modern cryogenic vessels and closed loops operate under hard vacuum, extreme radiation fields and cyclic thermal stresses. Low-temperature fluids, in particular helium-II in the quantum superfluid state and liquid hydrogen, possess anomalously high penetrability. These media diffuse through sub-micron defects of the crystal lattice and microcracks in welded joints (typical sizes ranging from a few nanometres to tens of micrometres) that remain inaccessible to ordinary gases and liquids.

Existing approaches rely on external leak detectors and polymer coatings. In the conditions of outer space, vacuum cryostats of fusion reactors or accelerator tunnels such methods are of limited effectiveness because of the structural inaccessibility of the components and radiation degradation of the coatings.

Known self-healing materials function predominantly inside a polymer matrix. NASA developed nanocomposites for composite cryogenic tanks (COPV) in which microcracks are healed upon moderate heating [1]. Systems based on ring-opening metathesis polymerisation (ROMP) with third-generation Grubbs catalysts demonstrate efficacy down to –20 °C and retain monomer-mixture fluidity to –70 °C [2, 3]. Self-healing at –20 °C has also been realised in protective coatings and elastomers [4, 5]. None of these approaches uses the working cryogen itself as the carrier of functional particles or provides sealing from inside an operating loop.

Nanoparticles are successfully dispersed in superfluid helium and employed as tracers without substantial disruption of superfluidity [6, 7].

Fluorine-containing polymers and oligomers possess structural integrity and radiation stability at cryogenic temperatures. Although polymers completely vitrify below 20 K, their sub-micron particulate forms are intended to act as rigid high-yield-strength blocking agents during the fluid transit phase. To overcome kinematic and phase constraints below 20 K, the invention architecture shifts from in-situ elastic deformation to a dual-stage temperature regime: mechanical capture during cryo-circulation followed by thermal activation during maintenance cycles. The following protective mechanisms are incorporated:

  • a sterically hindered nitroxyl inhibitor of radical polymerisation, covalently bound to the inner surface of the nanoshell, is intended to quench radiation-induced radicals within the closed volume and is inactivated only upon mechanical rupture of the capsule.
  • a low-boiling fluorinated oligomer configured for phase transition during thermal recovery; upon temperature modification, it spreads across the nascent crack facets to establish a high-adhesion polymeric anchor.

Kinetic constraints at 1.9–4.2 K are addressed by a hybrid mechanism with temporal and spatial separation of stages. In the first stage (at the working temperature of the loop) rapid physical condensation and adsorption of the fluorinated oligomer are anticipated. The resulting glassy plug is designed to seal the defect and preclude wash-out. Chemical cross-linking (including ROMP on highly active third-generation Grubbs ruthenium complexes and their modifications) is activated in a delayed manner—either during planned or forced warm-up of the sector to temperatures at which the catalyst becomes active (approximately –60 to –40 °C and above),

or by means of locally directed frontal or radiation-induced polymerisation without overall thermal recovery of the system. In this way the temporary physical plug is converted into a permanent chemically cross-linked polymer seal. Additional stimuli—a local electric field or weak external action—are also possible.

To limit radiation degradation and spatial isomerisation of the ruthenium complexes in zones of extreme ionising radiation (tokamak blanket, regions near targets and collimators of accelerators), nanodisperse aromatic energy acceptors (radiation protectors) are additionally introduced into the core. They are intended to provide non-destructive dissipation of quantum energy without alteration of the catalyst ligand configuration.

The structural rigidity of the vitrified Grade A shells at cryogenic temperatures ensures that the capsules remain intact during transit through pumps, valves, and filters. Geometric trapping and physical fragmentation are restricted exclusively to the extreme spatial confinement of the microdefect geometry, thereby preventing false operational triggering.

Anisotropic fluorosiloxane polymersomes are synthesised predominantly in the geometry of ellipsoids of revolution. This geometry ensures passive orientation of the rigid particles along the streamlines of the coolant, minimisation of the midship section, and reduction of local cavitation together with any increase in the normal-component fraction of helium-II.

Circulation is controlled by external sectionalised solenoids operating in an impulse-synchronised mode. A field gradient of 1–10 T m⁻¹ at a distance of several centimetres is estimated to be sufficient to retain the superparamagnetic particles. The local fields are substantially weaker than the working fields of superconducting magnets. With proper shielding and sectionalisation the influence on the main magnetic system is expected to remain minimal. Impulse synchronisation is intended to prevent establishment of a stable near-wall particle layer and to limit thermomechanical perturbations and internal counterflow in He-II.

For space applications the binary Grade B system employs combined electrostatic and steric stabilisation: capsules of opposite surface electrokinetic (zeta) potential together with outer shells covalently modified by branched perfluoroalkyl chains that form an entropic barrier against coagulation. Forced approach and activation are expected to occur only under the extreme geometric confinement inside a crack. As an alternative configuration for ultra-long-duration missions, spatial separation of the binary components is provided.

At particle volume fractions of 10⁻⁴–10⁻³ the change in effective viscosity of the normal component and thermal conductivity of helium-II is estimated at less than 1 %, consistent with experimental data on dispersion of nanoparticles ranging from 5 nm to 1 µm in He-II [6, 7]. These figures remain subject to confirmation under the specific conditions of the proposed suspensions.

3. Architecture and Specification of Base Agents

Three base physico-chemical modifications of the nanocomposites are declared.

Modification Grade A (pressure-responsive agent).

Hollow carbon or polymer nanospheres 15–50 nm in diameter containing a fluorine-containing monomer or oligomer, structurally optimized for compressive or tribological retention and rupture under geometric confinement inside microdefects.

The Grade A nanospheres exhibit high structural stability under bulk hydrodynamic shear and microcavitation pulses. At 1.9–4.2 K, the vitrified shells undergo tribological fragmentation or geometric jamming exclusively within the extreme spatial confinement of the microdefect channel, generating a dense physical plug. This initial compression retards substance migration until the scheduled maintenance cycle, where thermal evolution restores shell viscoelasticity, initiates core mobility, and drives targeted polymerisation. Alternative implementations include gas-filled hollow nanoparticles and phase-change cores. Radiation-protector energy acceptors are incorporated into the core.

Modification Grade B (binary dry agent).

A dual system of two types of nanospheres. Capsules B-1 contain an ultrafine dry powder of an acrylate or other polymer group; capsules B-2 contain a dry microcrystalline hardener. Interaction is activated exclusively by mechanical compression and shear deformations inside a narrowing crack channel of width 0.1–10 µm.

For space applications (hydrogen tanks of rocket stages) capsules B-1 and B-2 are additionally modified so as to possess opposite signs of the surface electrokinetic (zeta) potential in liquid hydrogen. In the bulk of the propellant they mutually repel. In addition, electrostatic stabilisation is duplicated by a steric factor: the outer shells are covalently modified with branched perfluoroalkyl chains that form an entropic barrier against coagulation. Forced approach and activation are expected to occur only under the extreme geometric confinement inside a crack.

As an alternative configuration for ultra-long-duration space missions, spatial separation of the binary system is declared, in which agent B-1 is dispersed in the fluid while component B-2 is impregnated onto the external surfaces of the tank structural elements.

Modification Grade C (piezoelectric catalytic agent).

Single-component nanospheres containing a metallo-organic complex. Activation occurs under the action of a local electric field generated by the deformed piezoelectric underlayer of the crack walls.

All modifications may be doped with superparamagnetic Fe₃O₄ particles 5–20 nm in size. The agent volume required for a typical defect (10⁻¹⁵–10⁻¹² L) at a concentration of 0.05 wt% is expected to be achieved by multiple passages of the flow.

4. Integration into Existing Equipment

Target niche: Large Hadron Collider, ultra-high-field medical MRI systems, research tokamaks and stellarators.

Principal engineering barriers: impossibility of altering loop geometry, presence of standard fine-mesh filters, risk of abrasive wear, tendency of ordinary lipid membranes to glassify below 20 K, risk of local heat release and quench.

Technological protocol.

Polymersomes based on block copolymers with fluorosiloxane segments are employed. While these materials exhibit a glass transition temperature (Tg) around 150 K and remain rigid at 1.9–4.2 K, their nanoscale dimensions and anisotropic ellipsoidal geometry minimize localized cavitation and ensure passive orientation along the streamlines of the coolant. The polymersomes are structurally optimized against premature degradation under bulk flow shear. Inside the microdefect, extreme geometric confinement forces the particles to fragment or lock together. This primary obstruction functions via mechanical friction and steric packing, preventing agent displacement until the scheduled thermal recovery restores the rubber-like state to initiate viscoelastic relaxation and adhesive wetting.

Circulation is controlled by external sectionalised solenoids with gradient management. A field gradient of 1–10 T m⁻¹ at a distance of several centimetres is estimated to be sufficient to retain the superparamagnetic particles. The local fields are substantially weaker than the working fields of superconducting magnets. With proper shielding and sectionalisation the influence on the main magnetic system is expected to remain minimal.

To limit thermomechanical effects and internal counterflow in He-II an impulse-synchronised operating mode of the solenoids is employed. The magnetic field is switched on in short high-frequency pulses (whenever possible synchronised with beam passage or periods of minimal thermal load). This is intended to prevent the near-wall particle layer from establishing a stable local thermal fluctuation. During scheduled maintenance the field may be switched off completely and the nanocomposite enters free circulatory flow. The solenoid profile is calculated so as to form a rarefied near-wall layer without abrupt flow constrictions.

5. Integration into Newly Designed Equipment

Target niche: future circular collider FCC, DEMO and PROTO reactors, interplanetary spacecraft with cryogenic stages, long-distance superconducting power lines.

The design documentation incorporates rejection of fine-mesh screen filters. Purification is transferred to contactless vortex hydrocyclones and electromagnetic traps that do not impede transit of sub-micron capsules.

Internal surfaces of the manifolds and critical zones of welded joints may, at the stage of metallurgical rolling or additive manufacturing, be modified with a thin-film piezoelectric underlayer. A nascent crack itself generates a potential that initiates activation of the Grade C agent.

For industrial volumes of Grade A, laboratory methods are supplemented by high-pressure jet co-extrusion in microfluidic chips and approaches based on hollow nanoparticles with controlled internal pressure.

6. Risk Analysis and Mitigation Measures

Potential vulnerabilities are addressed at the architectural level of the invention. The following constructive measures are intended to mitigate the principal engineering constraints; their effectiveness remains subject to experimental confirmation.

Local heat release in the hybrid mechanism is limited by the small agent volume and is scheduled to occur strictly during the controlled maintenance recovery phase, completely eliminating the risk of operational magnet quench. Sealing products may be removed by subsequent magnetic separation. The influence on superfluidity during the circulation stage at volume fractions up to 10^-3 is estimated as negligible on the basis of existing data. Compatibility with liquid hydrogen and structural materials (NbTi, Nb3Sn, copper, stainless steel) is pursued through the chemical inertness of the fluorine-containing components. The mechanical energy released during capsule structural fragmentation inside the defect is calculated to be negligible, preventing any substantial crack growth.

The structural rigidity of the vitrified shells at cryogenic temperatures, combined with the anisotropic ellipsoidal geometry of the polymersomes, ensures that the capsules remain intact during transit through pumps, valves, and filters. Fragmentation and physical trapping are restricted exclusively to the extreme spatial confinement of the microdefect geometry, thereby preventing false operational triggering.

The dual-stage temperature profile utilizes geometric jamming of the vitrified phase to isolate the leak path immediately at cryogenic temperatures. This solid-state restriction ensures mechanical retention of the composite within the defect geometry prior to the chemical cross-linking phase during scheduled maintenance (planned warm-up, local frontal or radiation-induced polymerisation). Premature wash-out is thereby reduced.

Impulse-synchronised operation of the sectionalised solenoids with a field gradient of 1–10 T m⁻¹ is intended to prevent establishment of a stationary near-wall layer, suppress thermomechanical perturbations and limit internal counterflow in He-II. Influence on the principal superconducting magnets remains minimal through shielding and sectionalisation.

Radiation-protector energy acceptors and the sterically hindered nitroxyl inhibitor are incorporated to suppress premature polymerisation inside intact capsules and to protect the catalyst ligand configuration against radiolysis in high-flux zones.

Combined electrostatic and steric stabilisation (opposite zeta potentials plus branched perfluoroalkyl chains), together with the option of spatial separation of Grade B components, is intended to exclude long-term coagulation in liquid-hydrogen tanks.

The agent volume required for a typical defect is expected to be achieved by multiple flow passages at concentrations not exceeding 0.05 wt%. Anisotropic particle shape and magnetic guidance are intended to promote efficient accumulation in the near-wall region without marked increase of hydrodynamic resistance.

These architectural provisions address the identified risks at the design stage. Subsequent stages serve for quantitative refinement of parameters already embedded in the concept and for experimental validation.

7. Directions for Further Research and Road-Map

To transition the declared self-sealing methodology from a conceptual framework to verifiable engineering protocols, a systematic research program is required. Future developments are categorized into three interdependent research clusters, culminating in an integrated technology validation timeline.

7.1. Phase 1: Computational Physics and Multiphase Modelling

Dual-Stage Kinetic Modelling: Advanced formulation of continuum mechanics and particle-dynamics models to decouple the sealing sequence. Investigations must isolate: (a) the low-temperature phase (1.9–20 K) governed by steric jamming, geometric trapping, and tribological fragmentation of vitrified nanocapsules under extreme spatial confinement; and (b) the high-temperature phase (above the glass transition temperature, Tg, of 150 K) characterizing matrix warm-up, polymer relaxation, catalyst activation, and subsequent frontal polymerisation kinetics.

Tribological and Shear Stability Assessment: Hydrodynamic simulation of capsule survival boundaries under extreme localized shear stresses. Models must establish structural thresholds for vitrified capsule geometries passing through high-velocity zones, specifically targeting the impellers and blades of cryogenic circulation pumps to prevent premature bulk disintegration.

Evacuation and Wash-Out Dynamics: Numerical evaluation of mechanical retention metrics during the transient phase between coolant evacuation and vacuumization. Research must quantify the critical packing density and friction coefficients required to mitigate particle wash-out driven by rapid gas desorbing streams prior to matrix thermal relaxation.

Electromagnetic Field Interactions: Structural and magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) modeling of superparamagnetic Fe3O4-doped capsule behavior under extreme external magnetic fields (5–12 T) and gradients characteristic of superconducting magnet configurations in particle accelerators and fusion reactors. Simulations must define pulse-synchronization profiles to suppress non-controlled near-wall inline particle aggregation and cluster formation.

7.2. Phase 2: Macromolecular Synthesis and Characterization

Block Copolymer Optimization: Controlled synthesis and structural characterization of tailored fluorosiloxane block copolymers. Research must focus on refining macroscopic yield strength while maintaining a highly reproducible low glass transition temperature (Tg ≈ 150 K) and fabricating anisotropic (ellipsoidal) configurations to maximize hydrodynamic alignment.

Interfacial Rheology of Vitrified Shells: Micro-mechanical testing of shell viscoelastic profiles and fracture toughness under variable cryo-pressure gradients. Experiments must correlate shell thickness-to-diameter ratios with predictable mechanical failure thresholds inside sub-micron fissures.

Colloidal and Steric Stabilization Protocols: Quantitative determination of electrokinetic (zeta) potentials and entropic barriers provided by branched perfluoroalkyl chains. Formulations must guarantee complete dispersion stability and eliminate premature self-coagulation within both liquid hydrogen matrices and superfluid helium environments.

Radiolytic Degradation Mitigation: Empirical verification of Third-Generation Grubbs catalyst integrity when shielded by nanodisperse aromatic energy acceptors. High-flux radiation benchmark tests (gamma and neutron exposure profiles) must confirm the prevention of ligand spatial isomerization and radiolysis.

7.3. Phase 3: Cryogenic Bench Testing and System Validation

Controlled Microdefect Simulation: Experimental validation utilizing specialized cryogenic optical cryostats equipped with piezo-actuated substrate displacement to simulate dynamic microcrack propagation (0.1–10 µm width) at 1.9 K and 4.2 K.

In-Line Sealing Verification: Closed-loop flow loop testing to measure real-time pressure drop recovery and mass-spectrometer leak rate reductions during agent accumulation. Tests must verify sealing efficiency across standard structural alloys, including NbTi, Nb3Sn, high-purity copper, and Austenitic stainless steels.

Operational Compatibility Mapping: Long-term exposure analysis to assess the impact of circulating agents on the effective viscosity of the normal fluid component, localized thermal conductivity of He-II, and total hydrodynamic resistance within fine-tolerance conduits.

7.4. Technology Readiness Roadmap

The development pathway follows a structured validation sequence:

Stage 1: Conceptual formulation and establishment of defensive worldwide priority.

Stage 2: Computational fluid modeling and laboratory-scale macromolecular synthesis.

Stage 3: Cryogenic bench testing inside customized optical cryostats down to 1.9 K.

Stage 4: Pilot loop integration and field validation inside operational accelerator or fusion subsystems.

8. Claims

A method of automatic Autonomous Cryogenic Two-Stage In-Loop Sealing (ACTIS) of microdefects in cryogenic loops, characterised in that the working coolant or liquefied propellant contains a suspension of functional nanocapsules at a concentration not exceeding 0.05 wt% (volume fraction phi approx. 10^-4 to 10^-3), operating via a dual-stage temperature regime:

(a) a low-temperature transport and capture stage at the working temperature of the loop (1.9–20 K), wherein the vitrified nanocapsules undergo mechanical jamming or geometric trapping directly inside the microdefect geometry under fluid flow pressure, forming a primary temporary physical restriction; and

(b) a subsequent delayed activation stage realized during planned or forced warm-up of the loop sector (above 150 K), wherein structural relaxation of the capsules occurs, initiating localized core release, adhesive wetting, and stimulated polymerisation (including ROMP on highly active third-generation Grubbs-type ruthenium catalysts protected by radiation-protector energy acceptors) to convert the temporary restriction into a permanent cross-linked polymer seal.

The method according to claim 1, characterised by the use of fluorosiloxane polymersomes of predominantly anisotropic (ellipsoidal) shape exhibiting structural rigidity at the transport stage and viscoelastic relaxation at the activation stage, managed by external magnetic separators with a field gradient of the order of 1–10 T m-1 operating predominantly in an impulse-synchronised mode, for existing systems without alteration of their geometry.

The method according to claim 1, characterised by equipping newly designed loops with contactless vortex separators and, where required, a piezoelectric underlayer on the internal surface of the pipes.

A composition of the nanocomposite agent for the ACTIS method according to claim 1, containing a sterically hindered nitroxyl inhibitor and, depending on the modification, a fluorinated oligomer anchor and radiation-protector energy acceptors; the composition is realised in a pressure-responsive variant with a nanostructural shell engineered for mechanical jamming and compressive rupture under geometric confinement, a binary dry variant with combined electrostatic and steric stabilisation by branched perfluoroalkyl chains or spatial separation of the components for space applications, or a piezo-activated variant, including combinations thereof.
